\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Device comparisons with people}
Sisti \emph{et al.} adapted a Wacom tablet spiral analysis to an iPad and compared the devices in 15 healthy people and 16 patients~\cite{Sisti2017}.
The iPad recorded a finger or a capacitive stylus at 60~Hz or less.
The nine spiral indices correlated between the devices, with $R^2$ from 0.52 to 0.90.
The study had no mechanical reference and no equivalence test.
In signature verification, a database of 65 people on five commercial devices was built to test verification between devices~\cite{Tolosana2017}.
These studies show that devices differ.
They cannot show how much of the difference is the measurement chain, because the person changes the movement too.

\subsection{Effects of the writing surface}
The surface changes how people write.
Children and adults wrote with different speed and different pauses on a tablet screen than on paper~\cite{Alamargot2015,Gerth2016a,Gerth2016b}.
For a comparison between an iPad and a Wacom tablet, this effect has two consequences.
A study with people measures the sum of the measurement chain difference and the behaviour difference.
A bench study measures the measurement chain difference only.
Both are needed, and neither replaces the other.

\subsection{Mechanical references for digitisers and other sensors}
Early work described the digital recording and processing of handwriting movements with a digitiser~\cite{Teulings1984}.
The pen industry tests jitter, linearity, and position accuracy with a motion system that moves the pen~\cite{Haran2014}.
Those tests check one product against its specification.
They do not compare the features that health research uses between products.
In the dysgraphia case of Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, the pressure signals of two tablets were compared with weights on a vertical pen~\cite{Asselborn2019}.
That test covered pressure at rest, not movement.
In human-computer interaction, dedicated apparatus measures the latency of touch and pen input~\cite{Ng2012,Casiez2017}.
Latency is a different quantity from the features that we test.

For wearable accelerometers, bench comparisons are common.
White \emph{et al.} moved 40 devices of four brands on a shaker table and tested their equivalence~\cite{White2024}.
Lamunion \emph{et al.} compared five generations of one research accelerometer on an orbital shaker against a calibrated reference~\cite{Lamunion2025}.
Our study applies the same idea to pen digitisers.
The camera takes the role of the calibrated reference, and the programmed drawing movements take the role of the shaker.

\subsection{Device as a confounder of screening models}
A model can learn the recording device in place of the disease.
In chest radiographs from three hospital systems, the hospital alone predicted pneumonia with an AUC of 0.86, because the disease rate differed between hospitals~\cite{Zech2018}.
In hip radiographs, the scanner model was predictable from the image, and the fracture model fell to chance when such variables were balanced~\cite{Badgeley2019}.
In smartphone studies, features of data quality alone told the operating system apart with an accuracy of 0.91~\cite{Halabi2024}.
Such shortcuts are a general property of trained models~\cite{Geirhos2020}.

A second error is leakage between the training set and the test set~\cite{Kapoor2023}.
When records of one person enter both sets, a model can recognise the person and give apparent performance that is too high~\cite{Saeb2017,ChaibubNeto2019}.

A third line of work treats a device change as measurement error in a predictor.
Simulations showed that a model loses discrimination and calibration when the predictors are measured less precisely at validation than at development~\cite{Luijken2019}.
Our consequence experiment follows this line.
Its inputs are not assumed: they are the bias and the noise that the bench measures.
