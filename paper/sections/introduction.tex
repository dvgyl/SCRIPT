\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
The way a person draws or writes carries information about motor and cognitive function.
A digitising tablet records the pen position many times per second.
Software then computes features such as the duration of a movement, its speed, and the strength of a tremor.
Researchers use these features to study Parkinson's disease, essential tremor, cognitive impairment, developmental disorders, and emotional states~\cite{Impedovo2019,Thomas2017,Elble2017,Toffoli2025,Asselborn2018,LikformanSulem2017}.
Many studies train a model that separates cases from controls.
We call such a model a \emph{screening model}.

Each feature depends on the device that records the movement.
For two decades, the usual device was a graphics tablet with an electromagnetic pen.
A review of public handwriting datasets for neurodegenerative disease lists three datasets recorded with Wacom tablets and one recorded with a sensor pen~\cite{Vessio2019}.
In a review of 120 studies of the digitised spiral test, 41 studies used a Wacom tablet and six used an iPad~\cite{Wang2025}.
Newer studies also use consumer tablets.
One study recorded 448 children with an Apple Pencil on an iPad~\cite{Asselborn2020}.
A norm, a threshold, or a model from one device may then be applied to data from another device.

This practice assumes that the two devices measure the same movement in the same way.
The assumption is rarely tested, and a failure can go unnoticed.
One case shows the risk.
A study recorded children with dysgraphia on one tablet model and other children on a newer model of the same brand~\cite{Asselborn2018}.
A published comment questioned whether the result was independent of the tablet model~\cite{Deschamps2019}.
The authors replied with a mechanical calibration of the pressure signal of the two tablets~\cite{Asselborn2019}.
The question could not be settled from the recordings of the children alone, because device and diagnosis were tied together.

Comparisons with people cannot separate two effects.
The first effect comes from the measurement chain: the stylus, digitiser, operating system, capture application, and analysis software.
Measurement chains can report different values for one movement.
The second effect is behaviour: a person moves differently on smooth glass than on a textured tablet or on paper.
Adults wrote loops about 20\,\% faster on a pen display than on paper~\cite{Gerth2016b}.
One earlier study compared an iPad with a Wacom tablet for spiral drawing in 31 people~\cite{Sisti2017}.
It used a finger or a capacitive stylus, not an active pen, and it reported correlations between the devices.
A correlation does not show agreement: two devices can correlate strongly and still differ by a constant factor.
We found no published study that drives an Apple Pencil and a Wacom pen with the same mechanical movement.

For other sensors, a mechanical reference is established practice.
Accelerometers of consumer watches and research devices have been compared on shaker tables~\cite{White2024,Lamunion2025}.
The verification, analytical validation, and clinical validation (V3) framework for digital health technologies calls this step \emph{verification}~\cite{Goldsack2020}.
Verification compares the sensor output against a bench reference before any test in people.
For drawing features, we found no such step for a graphics tablet and a consumer tablet with an active pen.

This paper describes a bench method for this step and applies it.
A motion system draws the same programmed movements on an iPad Pro and a Wacom Intuos Pro tablet.
The respective pens are an Apple Pencil Pro and a Wacom Pro Pen 3.
A camera at 240 frames per second records the movement that the stylus really makes in every run.
We ask four questions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Agreement.} Do the two devices give equivalent values for the same movement for each of six drawing features?
The equivalence limit is fixed in advance.
\item \emph{Correction.} Where they do not, does a simple correction learned on the bench remove the difference on mountings that the fit did not use?
\item \emph{Description.} What are the sample interval, the noise floor, and the spatial accuracy of each device during these movements?
\item \emph{Consequence.} How much does a device difference of the measured size change the apparent performance of a screening model?
How much of that change does the correction remove?
\end{enumerate}

The paper makes four contributions.
First, it gives a bench method for comparing pen digitisers.
The method has programmed movements, a camera reference with its own length standard and clock, and replication over mountings.
It tests equivalence against equivalence limits that are fixed before the main runs.
Second, it ties an equivalence limit to a consequence that a user of the features can judge.
The consequence is the largest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) that the device difference alone can produce.
The AUC measures how well a screening model separates cases from controls.
Three of the six equivalence limits follow this rule, and three are stated judgements.
Third, it gives a cross-device correction with a lack-of-fit check.
Fourth, it measures the consequence of the device difference in generated cohorts under three study designs, with and without the correction.

The scope is narrow by design.
The study tests one unit of each device, at one stylus angle and load, at slow to moderate speeds.
The results therefore describe these two units with our capture application, not the two product lines.
The study measures only the measurement chain.
It does not test the pressure, the tilt, or the latency of the devices.
It has no participants, and it makes no claim about people, about health, or about the accuracy of any screening model.
